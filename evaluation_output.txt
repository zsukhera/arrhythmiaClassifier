POTENTIAL EXPLANATION
---------------------
In a random 80/20 split, beats from the SAME patient appear in both
training and test sets. This means the model sees the patient's own
amplitude range, baseline wander pattern, and heart rate during
training. When it sees that patient again at test time, recognition
is easy.

In other words: this split is "easy" because the model is partially
learning who the patient is, and patient identity is a strong
predictor of future beats from the same patient. The model exploits
absolute amplitude features (fid_r_amp, fid_q_amp, etc.) which are
highly patient-specific. On this split, that leakage helps rather
than hurts.

Our result exceeding the paper is not cause for concern — it is
expected. It confirms the feature pipeline and SMOTE+LightGBM
setup are correctly implemented. The CV F1 (0.9864) closely
matches the held-out test F1 (0.9875), so the model is not
overfitting in the traditional sense — it is simply exploiting
patient-identity information that this split exposes.

  L  (LBBB)   : F1 = 0.000  — completely missed

LBBB is a zero. RBBB is effectively a zero. The model predicts

There is also a crucial contradiction: the CV F1 on the DS1
training set is 0.9856 — nearly identical to the random split.
The model is NOT underfitting during training. It collapses
entirely at test time. This combination (high train CV + low
test) is the classic signature of distribution shift, not
capacity failure.

POTENTIAL EXPLANATION
---------------------
This is an inter-patient generalisation failure. The DS1/DS2
split is the clinically correct way to evaluate: DS1 contains
one group of patients for training, DS2 contains a completely
different group for testing. No patient appears in both.

Three mechanisms combine to cause this failure:

1. ABSOLUTE AMPLITUDE FEATURES
   Features like fid_r_amp (R-wave peak amplitude), fid_q_amp
   (Q-wave amplitude), fid_s_amp (S-wave amplitude), and the
   wavelet energy bands all capture the absolute voltage scale
   of the signal. This scale is determined by electrode placement,
   body composition, and the patient's cardiac axis — none of
   which are arrhythmia-specific. When the model trains on DS1
   patients, it learns that "LBBB looks like a tall broad QRS
   at ~1.2 mV". DS2 patients may have LBBB with broad QRS at
   ~0.6 mV (different electrode placement). The model never
   saw that and classifies it as Normal.

2. LBBB AND RBBB ARE MORPHOLOGY-DEPENDENT
   Left Bundle Branch Block and Right Bundle Branch Block are
   diagnosed by the SHAPE of the QRS complex (widened, notched,
   with specific lead patterns) — not by amplitude alone. The
   exact morphological signature varies significantly between
   patients depending on the degree of block, co-existing
   conditions, and lead placement. When the amplitude scale
   is allowed to encode patient identity, the model cannot
   distinguish morphology from identity across the patient
   boundary.

3. SMOTE REINFORCES PATIENT-SPECIFIC DISTRIBUTIONS
   SMOTE synthesises new minority-class examples by interpolating
   between existing ones. If all LBBB training examples come from
   DS1 patients with a specific amplitude range, SMOTE creates
   more synthetic LBBB beats within that same amplitude range.
   It does not help generalise to the different amplitude scale
   of DS2 patients — it doubles down on DS1's distribution.

PVC (V) partially works (F1=0.652) because PVC is characterised
by a very wide, bizarre-looking beat that disrupts the rhythm
strongly enough to be detected even across patients. Its signal
is harder to mask by amplitude differences alone.

Normal beats (F1=0.835) work acceptably because they are the
majority class and the model defaults to predicting Normal when
uncertain, which is correct most of the time.

THE FIX
-------
Normalise each beat's amplitude features relative to the
record's own median R-amplitude before feature extraction.
This makes the features record-relative ("the R-wave is 1.2x
the record median") instead of absolute ("the R-wave is 1.2 mV").
Similarly, normalise RR intervals to the patient's mean RR
instead of using raw millisecond values. These two changes
remove patient identity from the feature vector, forcing the
model to learn arrhythmia morphology rather than patient
signatures.

POTENTIAL EXPLANATION
---------------------
A1 UNDERPERFORMANCE (6 statistical features)
The 6 statistical features are: mean, std, skew, kurtosis, energy,
and zero-crossing rate of the raw beat signal. These are very
coarse global descriptors. The paper reports 0.985 accuracy with
just these 6 features — which is actually very high. This suggests
either:
  (a) The paper's definition of "statistical features" includes
      slightly different or more discriminative quantities than ours
  (b) The paper's preprocessing pipeline differs in a way that makes
      the raw signal statistics more informative
  (c) The paper may be using a different feature set labelled
      "statistical" that includes timing information our A1 omits

Our A1 hitting 0.924 is not a disaster — it is reasonable for 6
features — but the gap to the paper (0.061) is meaningful and
suggests our statistical features are a weaker baseline than the
paper's.

A2 LARGE JUMP — FIDUCIAL FEATURES DOMINATE
Adding fiducial features (R amplitude, Q amplitude, S amplitude,
P-wave area, T-wave area, QRS width, Q-T interval, PR interval,
etc.) in our implementation gives an enormous boost. These features
are very discriminative on the random split because:
  - They directly encode QRS morphology (which differs by class)
  - They also encode patient-specific amplitude (which is
    consistent within a patient across the random split)

Both effects help on random split. The model exploits absolute
amplitude as a strong class discriminator within the patient
cohort it has already seen. This is why our jump (+0.070) is
so much larger than the paper's (+0.002) — the paper's model
either does not have this patient-identity shortcut available,
or uses normalised fiducial features that force it to learn
true morphology.

A3 AND A4 — MARGINAL GAINS
Wavelet features (A3, 31 total) and HRV/non-linear features
(A4, 40 total) add only tiny increments above A2:
  A3: +0.001 accuracy, +0.001 anomaly F1
  A4: +0.001 accuracy, +0.002 anomaly F1

This means on the random split, once you have fiducial features,
wavelet and HRV features add almost nothing. The model is already
at ceiling. On the DS1/DS2 split (not shown in ablation), wavelet
and HRV features would likely add more value if the amplitude
normalisation fix is applied — because they carry morphology
information that is less patient-specific.

KEY INSIGHT FROM ABLATION
The ablation exposes a contradiction: our model achieves ceiling
performance on the random split (exceeds paper significantly) but
collapses on DS1/DS2. The cause is the same: fiducial amplitude
features are too powerful on random split (because patient identity
leaks), and those same features cause failure on DS1/DS2 (because
a different patient's amplitudes are unrecognised). The ablation
confirms that these features are load-bearing for our results —
removing them (A1) drops us to 0.924, adding them (A2) jumps us
to 0.994. The fix must make these features patient-relative,
not remove them.
